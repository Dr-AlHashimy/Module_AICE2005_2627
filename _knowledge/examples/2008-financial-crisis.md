# 2008 Financial Crisis: Ignoring Systemic Risk

## The System

A financial system contains interconnected components: mortgages, banks, investment instruments, rating agencies, and regulators. These should function as a feedback-regulated system. Instead, each component was optimized independently.

## The Problem

**What happened:** Banks treated mortgages, derivatives, and complex financial instruments as isolated products. Each institution focused on maximizing its own returns without understanding how the parts interconnected.

**The feedback loop nobody saw:**
- Banks offer risky mortgages (high returns)
- Mortgages bundled into derivatives (complexity hides risk)
- Rating agencies give high ratings (incentives misaligned)
- Investors buy them (price reflects fake safety)
- Banks lend recklessly (no feedback about actual risk)
- Housing bubble inflates
- Bubble pops
- Derivatives become worthless
- Banks collapse
- Cascading failures across system

At each step, local decisions made sense. At the system level, it was a death spiral.

## Why Systems Thinking Was Missing

Banks optimized single components:
- Mortgage division: maximize lending volume
- Investment division: maximize returns on derivatives
- Each division had its own incentives and boundaries
- Nobody managed the system as a whole

The feedback loops were hidden:
- Risk was obscured by complexity
- Ratings gave false confidence
- Regulatory oversight was fragmented
- No one saw how interconnected the risk was

## The Consequence

- $16 trillion in wealth destroyed
- Millions lost homes
- Global recession
- Decades of economic damage

## System Theory Insight

A financial system with feedback loops between banks, mortgages, and investment instruments will exhibit emergent behaviour—in this case, catastrophic instability. You cannot prevent such crises by optimizing individual components. You need systemic regulation that manages feedback loops: capital requirements (negative feedback), risk transparency, interconnectedness limits.
