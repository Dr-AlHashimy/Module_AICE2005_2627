# Systems Thinking

## Definition

Systems thinking is the practice of using system theory as a lens to understand and solve complex problems. Rather than breaking problems into isolated parts, systems thinking examines relationships, feedback loops, delays, and emergent behaviours within the whole system. Key practices include mapping system structure and boundaries, identifying reinforcing and balancing feedback loops, recognizing non-linear cause-effect relationships, and tracing how changes propagate across the system over time.

Key characteristics are:
- Focus on relationships and interdependencies, not isolated components
- Recognition that problems emerge from system structure, not external causes alone
- Understanding delays and non-linearity—responses are not immediate or proportional
- Seeing patterns at the system level that cannot be predicted from parts in isolation

### Systems Thinking in Practice: The Suzanne Case

Suzanne, a senior manager in a regional health organisation, is mandated to reduce obesity. Her first instinct — a program to get people moving — treats the problem as an isolated part, ignoring the *whole* it sits within.

Obesity is a **complex system**: individual factors (physiology, habits, diet, occupation) interact with broader systemic factors (the built environment, food access, industry practices). A single linear program cannot fix a problem produced by interacting causes across levels.

**Zooming out** — looking beyond a program's own boundaries at policies, structures, and other interventions — lets Suzanne see **leverage points**: places in the system that can be nudged for outsized impact, some within her control, some not. A systems view doesn't change the program's boundaries, but it does expand the boundaries of its *evaluation*, capturing interrelationships a narrower evaluation would miss, even within a limited budget.

### Three Tools and Three Mindsets of Systems Thinkers

Across both management and design practice, systems thinkers reach for the same recurring moves.

Three tools:
- **Things *and* connections** — not just what a system is made of, but how its parts flow into each other (e.g. a university as a network of knowledge flow, not just departments and people).
- **Outflow, not just inflow** — asking not only how to acquire (talent, knowledge, resources) but how it's used, retained, or lost.
- **Cost of inaction, not just cost of action** — resources and risks accumulate invisibly (burnout, undocumented knowledge) until they cross a threshold; trends over time reveal this, a single snapshot doesn't.

Three mindsets, used alongside human-centred design:
- **Zoom in and out** — moving between a specific pain point and the larger whole it sits in (e.g. a single household's food waste vs. the whole food ecosystem).
- **Shift perspective** — viewing the problem through someone else's eyes, or borrowing an analogy from an unrelated context (food waste reframed as "old clothing" — discarded, but also donatable, repurposable).
- **Be aware of your own lens** — questioning the assumptions your own background and expertise bring, before they quietly limit the problem you think you're solving.

### Feedback Loops as a Model of Mind

Physicist Michio Kaku extends the feedback-loop idea as far as it will go: he defines **consciousness itself** as the sum total of feedback loops needed to build a model of yourself in space, society, and time — with a thermostat (one feedback loop) as the baseline unit.

| Level | Entity | Brain region | Domain modelled |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Plants | — | Temperature, humidity, gravity, sprouting |
| 1 | Reptiles · Robots | Reptilian (hindbrain) | Space |
| 2 | Social mammals | Limbic system (midbrain) | Society |
| 3 | Humans | Prefrontal cortex (forebrain) | Time — simulating the future |

Robots stay at Level 1: they perceive and react, but have no emotions or sense of hierarchy, and can only simulate the future in one narrow direction (e.g. airflow over a wing) rather than in all directions, as humans do. Kaku's related claim — that IQ tests mostly measure clerical skill, and real intelligence is the ability to simulate the future in all its forms — reframes "intelligence" itself as a feedback-loop property, not a test score.

## Why it matters

Systems thinking resists reductionism—the false assumption that understanding parts means understanding wholes. It is essential for engineers, researchers, and policymakers working with complex systems where local optimization can create system-level harm. In cyber-physical systems, software-driven organizations, and policy design, systems thinking prevents unintended consequences and reveals leverage points for effective intervention.
